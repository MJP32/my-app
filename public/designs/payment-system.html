System design

Payment System

The payments backend of a marketplace: take a buyer's money through a payment service provider, record every cent in a double-entry ledger, and pay the sellers out. Throughput is modest. The invariants are not: charge exactly once, and never create or lose a cent. Click through the phases the way you'd walk a whiteboard.

"Design a payment system" is a trap of breadth: cards, wallets, FX, subscriptions, fraud, disputes, tax, payouts in forty countries. You are not building Stripe. You are building the system that sits between a marketplace's checkout and a Stripe — and you say out loud which half of the problem you are deferring.

Functional requirements

The money movements to design end to end
  • Pay-in. A buyer pays for an order by card. We authorize through a PSP, capture when the order ships (or immediately for digital goods), and tell checkout the outcome.
  • Ledger. Every movement — capture, fee, refund, payout — is a balanced double-entry journal entry. Balances are derived from it, never stored as the source of truth.
  • Refunds. Full or partial, idempotent, never more than was captured, reflected in the ledger and the seller's balance.
  • Pay-out. Sellers are paid their available balance on a schedule to a bank account, minus the platform's commission.
  • Reconciliation. Every day, prove that our ledger, the PSP's settlement report and the bank statement agree — and raise a break when they don't.

The one-liner to open with

Say this before you draw a single box

"This is a low-throughput, high-stakes system. A few hundred payments a second is easy; charging someone twice, or losing a cent between three parties who all keep their own books, is not. So I'll design around three things: an idempotency key that travels from the buyer's click all the way into the PSP, a payment state machine where every transition is a guarded write, and an append-only double-entry ledger that a daily reconciliation proves against the outside world. Card numbers never touch our servers."

The estimate here has a punchline most candidates miss: payments are not a throughput problem. Ten million payments a day is about a hundred a second. The numbers exist to prove a single relational primary carries it — so you can spend the interview on correctness instead of sharding.

Where the time goes

Latency, not throughput
  • PSP authorization: 400 ms – 2 s, and the tail is the PSP's, not yours
  • 3-D Secure challenge: seconds to minutes, with a human in the loop
  • Your own writes: a few ms each — payment row, idempotency row, outbox row
  • So threads blocked on the PSP are the resource to budget, not CPU

Where the rows go

Storage, not traffic
  • Ledger lines are append-only and kept for years — the only table that grows without bound
  • Payments and refunds: one row each, mutated through a handful of states, then cold
  • PSP webhooks and raw responses: kept verbatim for disputes and audits, in S3
  • Settlement files: one per PSP per day, a few hundred MB, parsed into a recon table

The point of the estimate

Say this conclusion aloud

Peak is a few hundred payments a second and a few thousand ledger inserts — one well-sized Aurora writer with time partitioning carries that for years. I won't shard on day one. I will isolate the PSP calls behind a bulkhead, because a slow PSP holding every thread is the realistic outage, and I'll partition the ledger by month so seven years of history detaches to cold storage.

The contract is where exactly-once starts. The interviewer is listening for an Idempotency-Key on every money-moving POST, amounts as integer minor units with an explicit currency, 202 wherever the PSP decides later, and a webhook endpoint you treat as hostile until verified.

API surface

Three contract rules worth saying out loud

  • Money is an integer and a currency. {"amount": 10000, "currency": "USD"} — never 100.00, never a float, and JPY has zero decimal places so "minor units" is per-currency.
  • Same key, different body is a 422, not a replay. Otherwise a client bug that reuses a key silently returns the wrong payment.
  • The response names the state, not a boolean. REQUIRES_ACTION, PROCESSING, AUTHORIZED — "success: true" cannot describe a payment that has not finished yet.

One database per service and no table with two writers. The payment row is a state machine with a version; the ledger is append-only and has no UPDATE path at all; balances are a projection you can delete and rebuild.

Schemas

Pick a store

Schema decisions worth defending

  • Amounts are bigint minor units plus a char(3) currency. Not numeric with decimals, and never a float — 0.1 + 0.2 is not a refund you want to explain.
  • The payment row has a version, and every transition names the state it expects. A late webhook trying to move CAPTURED back to AUTHORIZED updates zero rows.
  • The ledger has no UPDATE or DELETE grant. Mistakes are corrected with a reversing entry. The database role enforces it, not a code review.
  • Balance = SUM(lines), and the stored balance in the projection carries the ledger sequence it was computed at, so staleness is measurable.
  • The PSP reference is unique — UNIQUE (psp, psp_payment_id) — so a webhook can never attach to the wrong payment or create a second one.
  • Cross-service references are plain IDs. A journal entry points at payment_id as a correlation, not a foreign key into another service's database.

The ledger is the heart of the system and the part interviewers probe hardest. Double-entry is one rule: every entry has lines that sum to zero, so money only ever moves between accounts — it is never created or destroyed. Post the events below and watch the trial balance stay at zero.

Rules the ledger enforces

Each one is a constraint, not a convention
  • Balanced or rejected. A deferred constraint trigger sums the lines of each entry at commit; anything but zero aborts the transaction.
  • Append-only. No UPDATE, no DELETE. A wrong entry is fixed by posting its exact reversal and then the correct one — both visible forever.
  • Idempotent posting. Every entry carries an idempotency_key such as capture:PAY-7f3a with a unique constraint, so a redelivered event cannot post twice.
  • Authorization is not a ledger event. An auth moves no money; it is tracked on the payment. The ledger starts at capture.
  • One currency per entry. FX is two entries through an FX clearing account, never a line that silently converts.

Posting an entry, in code

// one transaction: the entry, its lines, and the outbox event — or nothing
@Transactional
public EntryId post(JournalEntry e) {
    if (e.lines().stream().mapToLong(Line::signedAmount).sum() != 0)
        throw new UnbalancedEntry(e);                       // checked here AND by the DB trigger
    try {
        jdbc.update("INSERT INTO journal_entry(id, idempotency_key, kind, occurred_at) VALUES (?,?,?,?)",
                    e.id(), e.idempotencyKey(), e.kind(), e.occurredAt());
    } catch (DuplicateKeyException dup) {
        return existingEntryFor(e.idempotencyKey());          // replay: already posted, no-op
    }
    jdbc.batchUpdate("INSERT INTO journal_line(entry_id, account_id, direction, amount, currency) VALUES (?,?,?,?,?)",
                     e.lines(), e.lines().size(), Line::bind);
    outbox.put(new EntryPosted(e));
    return e.id();
}

A payment system is judged by what it does at 3 a.m. when a PSP is degraded. The metrics that matter are business metrics — authorization rate, unknown-state count, reconciliation breaks — not CPU.

Tracing one payment across three companies

The payment_id is the correlation ID — it is in every log line, every event, and the PSP metadata
  • Put payment_id in the PSP's metadata on create. Every webhook and every settlement line then carries your ID back, which turns reconciliation from fuzzy matching into a join.
  • Log the attempt, not just the payment. One payment may have three PSP calls; each attempt row stores request ID, latency and raw response pointer in S3.
  • Never log a PAN, CVV or full bank number. A log scrubber at the collector, plus a canary test that fails the build if a test card number appears in log output.

Disaster recovery

The money path fails over; the reporting path waits
  • Within a region: Aurora multi-AZ with RPO 0. Losing an AZ is a 30-second failover that in-flight payments absorb because every write is idempotent and retried.
  • Across regions: active-passive. Aurora Global Database (RPO ~1 s). Before taking writes in the secondary, run the resolver over every payment created in the last minutes of the primary — anything replicated as CREATED may already be charged at the PSP.
  • Why not active-active? Two regions both writing a seller's balance is a distributed ledger problem. Not worth it at a hundred payments a second; say so.

Traps in this specific problem

Where candidates lose the room
  • Storing a balance column and incrementing it. Two concurrent captures race, the number drifts, and nobody can say why the seller is owed $3.20 more than they should be. The balance is a sum of ledger lines.
  • Treating a timeout as a failure. The PSP may have charged the card. Retrying with a new key charges it again; marking it failed loses a sale the buyer paid for. It is UNKNOWN until the PSP tells you.
  • Floats for money. Or decimals in JSON. Integer minor units and a currency code, and a stated rounding rule for splits.
  • Two-phase commit across the PSP. The PSP will not join your transaction. Use a state machine, an outbox and compensations.
  • Trusting the webhook body. Verify the signature, then fetch the object from the PSP by ID. A webhook is a POST from the internet.
  • Card numbers on your servers. Hosted fields or the PSP SDK tokenize in the browser. You store a token and stay in SAQ-A.
  • Failing over to a second PSP mid-payment. If the first one is in doubt, a second charge is a double charge. Failover is for new payments.
  • No reconciliation. Without it, every bug above is silent. Recon is how you find out you were wrong before the auditor does.
