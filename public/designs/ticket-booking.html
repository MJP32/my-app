System design

Ticket Booking System

Fan-facing web and mobile platform, scoped to the four steps of the fan journey: search the ticket, select the seat, book the seat, do the payment. Fifty thousand seats and a million people who want them, so the seat is the product and the invariant is never sell one twice. Click through the phases the way you'd walk a whiteboard.

Spend the first five minutes cutting the problem down. A ticketing platform touches event management, pricing, resale, venue access, marketing and settlement — you cannot design all of it. This round keeps exactly four steps — search, select, book, pay — and says out loud what it is deferring.

Functional requirements

The four steps to design end to end
  • Search the ticket. Find events by city, date, artist and category, with on-sale status and a price-from, cached for seconds at the edge.
  • Select the seat. See a live seat map — geometry from the CDN, status as a bitmap, changes pushed over a socket — and pick specific seats.
  • Book the seat. Hold the seats for ten minutes while you pay. Two people can never hold the same seat; an abandoned booking releases itself on a clock.
  • Do the payment. Charge exactly once through a PSP that answers by webhook, flip the seats from held to sold under the hold that paid, and confirm.

The one-liner to open with

Say this before you draw a single box

"This is a read-heavy browsing site wrapped around a write-critical inventory. Reads I'll scale with a CDN, a cached seat map and a denormalized read model. Writes I'll keep tiny, strongly consistent and idempotent — a seat changes state through one guarded update, and I'd rather show a brief error than sell a seat twice. And the spike is a product of the calendar, so I know the second it arrives — reads that aren't requests and a hold budget at the edge carry it, and I'll name the waiting room as the next lever rather than pretend I built it."

Reading these diagrams

Standard UML, and worth being precise about — the two dashed relationships are the ones people get backwards
  • Stick figures are actors, and an actor is a role, not a person. The same human is a Fan searching and the same Fan paying, and the clock that expires their hold is an actor too.
  • Actors on the right are secondary — the system calls them, they don't call the system. Every one of them is a dependency you have to be able to fail without.
  • «include» points at behaviour that always happens. The arrow runs from the base use case to the included one. If it can be skipped, it isn't an include.
  • «extend» points the other way — from the optional behaviour back to the base, because the base doesn't know its extensions exist. Every extend has a condition attached.
  • The box is the system boundary, and drawing it is the point: everything outside is somebody else's uptime.
  • A time-triggered actor is still an actor. Hold expiry has no human initiator, which is exactly why candidates forget it exists — and why abandoned seats stay unsellable in their designs.

Where the reads go

Peak read budget
  • Seat-map geometry — CDN, immutable per event version, never hits an origin twice
  • Seat status — a 2-bit-per-seat bitmap in Redis, ~12 KB for a 50K-seat venue
  • Deltas — pushed over WebSocket from a Redis channel, so most "refreshes" are not requests at all
  • Event search — OpenSearch, read replicas, results cached for seconds

Where the writes go

Peak write budget
  • Hold attempts on one event — the only genuinely hot write, and all of it lands on one shard
  • Seat transitions: HELD → SOLD, HELD → AVAILABLE on expiry
  • Orders and payments — one per successful hold, orders of magnitude fewer than attempts
  • Ticket issuance and scans — spread across a venue's doors over an hour

The point of the estimate

Say this conclusion aloud

Year-round writes are trivial. One on-sale is the whole problem: everything lands on one event, which is one shard, in one minute. So partition inventory by event_id, cap how often one fan can try at the edge so losers cost a millisecond and not a retry storm, and serve every read from a copy that is not the primary.

What needs partitioning, what needs sharding, and what needs neither

When partitioning is not enough

A partitioned table still lives in one writer. If that writer runs out of headroom on the biggest on-sale of the year, the next step is sharding by event across clusters — the same key, one level up. It works here for the same reason partitioning does: no hold spans two events. And unlike most systems, you know the hot key in advance, so a mega on-sale is pinned to its own shard the week before.

// routing is a pure function of the key, resolved before the connection is chosen
Integer pin = pinned.get(eventId);                      // stadium tours get their own shard
int shard = pin != null ? pin : Math.floorMod(Long.hashCode(eventId), SHARD_COUNT);
DataSource ds = shards.get(shard);                      // inventory-shard-03
  • Say what it costs before you are asked. Cross-event queries stop existing, a shared unique constraint stops existing, and moving a live event between shards is an operation you do before the on-sale, never during.
  • Use a virtual-bucket layer. Map events to 1,024 buckets and buckets to shards; growing then means moving buckets, not rehashing every event.
  • Do not shard the small tables. Events, venues and price tiers ride along with their service; only seats and the seat-event log are candidates, because they are the only ones whose growth is unbounded.
The honest answer for this system: a metered on-sale delivers 2,000–5,000 holds a second to one partitioned Aurora cluster, which it carries comfortably. Sharding is the plan you describe for the day two stadium tours go on sale at the same minute — and knowing which of those you are proposing is most of the question.

Three schema decisions worth defending

  • Seat status is a row with a version, and every transition is an UPDATE whose WHERE clause names the state it expects. Zero rows updated is the answer "you lost", and no application code ever reads-then-writes.
  • Money is bigint minor units, never a float, and prices are copied onto the order at hold time so a tier repricing during the on-sale cannot change what a fan was quoted.
  • Availability is derived. The seat-map bitmap is a cache with a version, rebuildable by folding the seat table, and a nightly job proves it still matches.
  • Every outbox lives beside the data it describes, so the event and the row commit together or neither exists.
  • Cross-service references are plain uuids, not foreign keys. An order references a hold that lives in another service's database, and pretending otherwise is how a boundary quietly dissolves.
  • Partial indexes on the hot set — WHERE status = 'HELD' — so the expiry sweeper scans the few thousand live holds, not the history of every on-sale.

Command Query Responsibility Segregation is one sentence: the model you write with and the model you read from are different models, kept in step by an event rather than by a shared table. Here the write model is a seat row with a version and the read model is a bitmap — and neither can do the other's job.

The split, drawn out

Two models, one event between them, and no call in either direction

How it is used here

Not as an abstraction — as three concrete pieces you can point at
SideComponentsWhat it optimises for
Command Reservation · Inventory · Payment, each with its own Aurora cluster Correctness at commit. One row per seat, a version column, a guarded UPDATE per transition, and an append-only seat_event log. A few thousand writes a second at the peak of an on-sale, and every one of them has to be right.
The seam Outbox table → relay → MSK One event per committed transition, published from the same transaction that wrote the row. Neither side calls the other, and neither imports the other's model.
Query Seat-map projector · ElastiCache · CloudFront · Event Page BFF · Search over OpenSearch Answering the exact question the screen asks: which seats can I click. Two bits per seat, pushed as deltas. A hundred thousand reads a second during an on-sale, none of which touch the write path.
Read the ratio again: 100,000 reads against 2,000 writes, and the reads are almost all the same 12 KB. Serving both from one model means sizing the correctness-critical path for the volume of the trivial one. The split is not elegance — it is what makes a row lock per seat affordable.

What it buys

  • Independent scaling. Add Redis replicas and WebSocket nodes without touching the inventory primary. The write side scales on holds, the read side on eyeballs, and those two numbers move for completely different reasons.
  • Different consistency per side. Strong where a seat is decided, eventual where a colour is painted — with the staleness stated rather than hidden.
  • Different storage shapes. The write model is a row with a version because the invariant lives there. The read model is a bitmap because a seat map is one shape. Neither compromise is forced on the other.
  • Failure isolation. Losing the read model greys out a map; losing the write model stops holds. One of those is a page and the other is a queue that keeps filling — and the split is what keeps them separate.
  • The read model is disposable. Wrong colour, new price tier, changed shape — reset the consumer group and replay. Rebuildable state is the underrated half of this pattern.
  • Query shapes can change freely. Adding a "seats left per tier" counter meant a new consumer and a new key, and zero changes to inventory.

What it costs, said before you are asked

  • Eventual consistency becomes a product decision. A fan can click a seat the map shows as free and be told it is gone. That is correct behaviour and it has to be designed as a graceful "pick another" rather than an error page.
  • Read-your-own-writes needs an explicit exception. After a hold, that fan must see their own seats as theirs, so the Reservation Service patches the bitmap synchronously before returning 201. One deliberate crack, for exactly one case.
  • More moving parts. An outbox, a relay, a topic, a projector, a Pub/Sub channel and a WebSocket tier — all of which need lag monitoring and a DLQ.
  • Two schemas to evolve. A new seat state in the write model is not automatically a colour in the bitmap, and the rebuild has to be part of the deploy rather than an afterthought.
When not to use it: for a community hall with 200 seats and no on-sale spike, CQRS is ceremony — read the seat table. It earns its keep at a ratio like this one, or when the read shape is genuinely different from the write shape. Saying that unprompted is worth more than the pattern name.

In code, the split is two packages that share nothing

// command side — the invariant, one seat at a time, one transaction for the whole hold
@Transactional
public HoldId handle(HoldSeats cmd) {
    for (SeatId seat : cmd.seats()) {
        int n = jdbc.update("UPDATE seat SET status='HELD', hold_id=?, version=version+1 "
                         + "WHERE event_id=? AND seat_id=? AND status='AVAILABLE'",
                         cmd.holdId(), cmd.eventId(), seat);
        if (n == 0) throw new SeatUnavailable(seat);          // rolls back every seat above
    }
    outbox.put(new SeatsHeld(cmd));                          // same tx, no dual write
    return cmd.holdId();
}

// query side — no domain objects, no invariants, just the shape the screen wants
public byte[] seatMap(EventId eventId) {
    return redis.get("seatmap:" + eventId);                   // 2 bits per seat, ~12 KB
}

// the seam — the only thing that connects them
@KafkaListener(topics = "inventory.seat-transitioned.v1")
public void on(SeatTransitioned e) {
    redis.bitfield("seatmap:" + e.eventId(), e.seatIndex() * 2, e.status().code());   // idempotent: same bits twice
    redis.publish("seatmap-delta:" + e.eventId(), e.toDelta());
}

The tell that it is real: the query side has no @Transactional, no version column, and no way to change a seat. If your read path can write, you have two models and one of them is lying.

Reading the diagram out loud

What each convention encodes — shape first, then colour, then line style
  • Solid arrows are synchronous calls — the caller blocks and owns the timeout.
  • Dashed arrows are responses or asynchronous returns that arrive later, sometimes minutes later — a PSP webhook, a seat-map delta.
  • Arrows point the way the call goes, not the way the data goes. The outbox relay points at the relational databases because it polls them. The projector points at Redis because it writes it.
  • Nothing writes to the inventory database directly except the Inventory Service. That is the single most important line on the board.
  • No service dual-writes to a database and Kafka. Everything goes database → outbox → relay → Kafka.
  • The two dashed bands at the bottom are not services. Observability and disaster recovery apply to every box above them, so they are drawn as bands rather than nodes — a node with no edges would be a lie about how it is wired.
  • The outbox is drawn inside the database, because that is where it lives — a table committed in the same transaction as the row it describes. The relay reads it; it is not a queue sitting between the two.
  • Every store sits directly opposite the one service that owns it. If you can draw a horizontal line between a service and a database, that service is the only thing connecting to it — no shared clusters, no second writer.
  • Green boxes marked AWS are managed services — someone else runs the failover. Everything else is code you deploy and page for.
  • The red box is the whole PCI scope. Card numbers go from the fan's browser to the PSP's vault and never through your services. Everything else handles a payment-method token.
  • Select a flow and an amber dashed box appears around the components only that flow uses. Everything left outside it is shared — which is exactly the set whose blast radius crosses flows, and the set you cannot change alone.
  • Kafka is partitioned by event_id, which is why per-event ordering holds without a global lock — a seat's HELD cannot overtake its AVAILABLE.

The dead letter path

What happens to a seat transition the projector will not process — and the harder question of what happens to the ones behind it

Transient — goes to the retry ladder

  • Redis timeouts, a connection reset, a 5xx from the notification vendor, broker unavailability, a lock contention rollback.
  • Non-blocking retry: the message moves to a separate retry topic with its own consumer and a backoff. It never sleeps in the handler, because a sleeping consumer holds a partition hostage — and a partition here is an event's entire seat map.
  • Backoff is exponential with jitter. A synchronized retry storm during an on-sale is how a struggling Redis becomes a dead one.

Permanent — straight to the DLT

  • Deserialization failures, schema incompatibility, a seat index outside the venue's map, an event the projector has never seen.
  • No retries at all. Burning six attempts on a message that can never parse is theatre — it delays the alert and fills the ladder with garbage.
  • The classifier is an explicit exception-to-decision map, not a catch-all. Anything unclassified is treated as permanent, because a mystery failure retried forever is worse than one that surfaces.

Operating it

  • One DLT per consumer group, never a shared bin. A shared queue means you cannot tell whose problem it is or replay one team's messages without replaying everyone's.
  • Alarm on depth and age. Depth catches a burst. Age catches the single message from Tuesday that nobody noticed — the more dangerous of the two.
  • Every dead-lettered message carries its own forensics in headers: original topic, partition, offset, exception class and message, attempt count, and the correlation ID that ties it back to the originating hold.
  • Replay is re-publishing to the source topic, not hand-patching a bitmap. It is only safe because consumers are idempotent by event ID — which is the reason that property is non-negotiable upstream.
  • The ordering trade-off is the real answer. Dead-lettering transition 3 and processing transition 4 breaks per-event order. For notifications that is fine — a receipt arriving before a reminder is survivable.
  • For the seat map it is not. A seat painted AVAILABLE after its SOLD was skipped is a fan clicking a seat that is gone. The projector halts its partition and pages, and the map for that event goes stale rather than wrong. Stale is a refresh; wrong is a support ticket.

Observability — the eleven-second checkout

One incident, walked end to end. The tooling, coverage and alerting policy are on the Observability tab.

One trace, pulled by correlation ID from the checkout a fan complained about — the one where the hold expired while the spinner was still going. Every span is real instrumentation you have to have decided on in advance; after the incident is too late.

Metrics — what you alert on

  • RED per endpoint — rate, errors, duration as histograms, never averages. A p99 is a number; an average is a rumour.
  • Prometheus scrapes, Grafana asks. Micrometer exposes the registry, Prometheus stores it, Grafana holds the dashboards and the alert rules. CloudWatch keeps the AWS-side metrics — Aurora connections, MSK consumer lag, ElastiCache evictions — that no application can report about itself.
  • Four that are specific to this system: oversell count (must be zero, always), hold-conflict rate, projector lag per event, and PSP webhook age.
  • Saturation, not just latency — the PSP client's in-flight count is the metric that would have caught the trace above before a fan did.

Logs — structured, and safe

  • JSON to stdout, one event per line, with traceId, spanId, eventId, holdId and idempotencyKey on every record.
  • Never a card number, a CVV, or a full PSP response body. Enforce it with a serializer that redacts by field name, not by developer discipline.
  • Retention split: 30 days hot for debugging, seven years in WORM for anything an order or a payment touched.

Traces — across the async gap

  • OpenTelemetry is the instrumentation layer — auto-instrumented for HTTP and JDBC, manual spans around the PSP call, the Redis bitfield and the outbox relay. The collector then fans out: traces to AWS X-Ray, metrics to Prometheus, logs to CloudWatch.
  • Context propagates through Kafka headers, so the projector's span links back to the hold that produced it. Without this, every trace stops at the outbox and "why is my seat still grey" becomes guesswork.
  • Tail-based sampling: keep 100% of errors and anything over the SLO, 1% of the rest — but 100% of anything during a flagged on-sale window.

What pages, and what doesn't

  • Page on symptoms. Any oversell at all, SLO burn on the hold path during an on-sale, projector lag past ten seconds on a live event, and PSP webhook age past the hold TTL. All four mean a fan is affected or a seat is unaccounted for.
  • Ticket on causes. A pod restart, a rising cache miss rate, a slow query. Paging on causes is how you train an on-call rotation to ignore alerts.
  • Every alert names an owner and links a runbook. An alert nobody owns is a dashboard with a siren attached.
  • The oversell check is the highest-signal alert in the system — a nightly fold of the seat_event log asserting every SOLD seat has exactly one PAID order. It is the only one that can tell you the invariant broke, which no latency metric ever will.

The four that carry the design

If you only defend a few choices, defend these — the rest are conventional
  • Aurora, for inventory. The invariant is one row changing state under a guard, and a multi-seat hold committing or rolling back together. That is a relational engine's job description. Every "why not DynamoDB for everything" question ends here.
  • ElastiCache, for the two things that must be fast and may be lost. The seat-map bitmap and the hold TTL are both derived or rebuildable. If Redis dies, holds still expire from the database sweeper and the map is rebuilt from the seat table.
  • MSK, for the seam. Retention is what makes the read model disposable. With a queue, a projector bug means a wrong seat map you cannot recover; with a log, it means a consumer group you reset.
  • CloudFront, quietly. Nobody asks about it and it removes the majority of on-sale traffic before it reaches anything you run. Naming the versioned-URL trick unprompted is a strong signal you have operated a launch.

Lambda, on the hold pathCold starts at the exact second an on-sale opens, and a connection-per-invocation model that fights the pool. Fine for a nightly job; wrong for the transaction that decides a seat.
Step FunctionsThe hold → pay → confirm saga is a state column plus events, debuggable in SQL. A managed orchestrator would move that state somewhere your service cannot query when a fan asks where their seats went.

The line to say: managed services are bought to remove operational work, not decisions. Every one of these still has a failure mode I have to design around — and the ones I refused, I refused for a reason I can state in a sentence.

Portability, since someone always asks

Roughly 85% of this design ports. Postgres, Kafka, Redis, OpenSearch and S3-compatible storage exist everywhere; the services behind them are ordinary Spring Boot. The lock-in is concentrated in three places, and it is worth knowing which:

  • Cognito — the real one. Hashes cannot be exported, so leaving means a forced reset or a lazy migration.
  • IAM and Lattice — the authorization model, not the code, is what would have to be rebuilt.
  • CloudFront's versioned invalidation model — any CDN does this, but the deploy pipeline is written against one of them.

Five tools, and the answer to the only question that matters when an on-sale is going wrong: which one do you open first? Instrument once with OpenTelemetry, then let each backend do the thing it is actually good at.

Tracing across the asynchronous gap

The part most designs get wrong, and the reason the traces are worth having at all
// producing: the trace context rides in the Kafka record headers
ProducerRecord<String, byte[]> rec = new ProducerRecord<>(topic, eventId, payload);
propagator.inject(Context.current(), rec.headers(),
    (h, k, v) -> h.add(k, v.getBytes(UTF_8)));      // traceparent, tracestate

// consuming: link the projector span back to the hold that caused it
Context parent = propagator.extract(Context.root(), record.headers(), GETTER);
Span span = tracer.spanBuilder("seatmap.project")
                  .setParent(parent)
                  .setSpanKind(CONSUMER)
                  .startSpan();

Without this, every trace stops at the outbox. You would see a 201 returned in 40 ms and have no way to connect it to the seat that stayed grey on every other fan's screen for a minute. With it, one correlation ID spans the hold, the relay, the topic, the projector and the WebSocket push.

What pages, and what does not

  • Page on symptoms. Any oversell, SLO burn on the hold path during an on-sale, projector lag on a live event, PSP webhook age past the hold TTL, and a reconciliation break over a threshold. Each one means a fan is affected or a seat is unaccounted for.
  • The oversell check is the highest-signal alert in the system — the only one that can tell you the invariant broke, which no latency metric will ever say.
  • Ticket on causes. A pod restart, a rising cache miss rate, a slow query. Paging on causes is how you train an on-call rotation to ignore alerts.
  • Every alert names an owner and links a runbook. An alert nobody owns is a dashboard with a siren attached.
Not on the list: CPU, memory, pod count. Those are causes, and causes belong in a dashboard you open after a symptom has already paged you.

Every pattern here is load-bearing — if it didn't earn a place in the architecture, it isn't on this list. The four highest-value ones are the ones nobody puts on a list: the guarded conditional update, transactional outbox, event-driven cache patching, and the choice of partition key. Smaller patterns worth naming in passing

Not worth a deep dive, but dropping the name shows you've built this before
  • Snapshotting. The seat-map bitmap is a memoized fold over the seat_event log. It is what makes a 50,000-seat map a 12 KB read instead of a query.
  • Compensating Transaction. A failed payment releases the seats with a new transition and marks the order FAILED, never an UPDATE that erases what was tried. In ticketing you don't undo — you post the opposite.
  • Bulkhead. Sits beside every circuit breaker. A slow PSP consumes a bounded pool instead of starving hold requests for the fans behind it.
  • Dead Letter Queue. With a retry topic and backoff in front of it. A DLQ nobody monitors is just a slower way to paint the wrong seat map.
  • Shadow / Dark Launch. A new seat-map projector runs beside the old one on a real on-sale before it is allowed to write the bitmap anyone reads.
  • Rate Limiting. Sliding window at the gateway, per fan and per device, with hold budgets far tighter than seat-map budgets.

Everything an interviewer asks after the design holds up: what is wrong with it, how do you know it works, how do you ship it, and what does it cost. Volunteering these reads as confidence; being asked for them and having nothing reads as the opposite.

What I would change, and what breaks at 10×

Said before anyone asks. Each one is a real weakness in the design as drawn, not a humble-brag
The weaknessBites atWhat I would do about it
No waiting roomA world-tour on-sale Rate limits and a hold budget shed losers, but they do not change the shape of the arrival. At a million fans in ten seconds the gateway and the hold path are absorbing a spike they could be metering. The next lever is a queue in front of the hold route with an admit rate tied to hold p99 — deferred here, and the first thing I would add.
Hold expiry depends on two mechanisms agreeingRedis failover Keyspace notifications are best-effort and lost on failover. The sweeper catches it, but at a 30-second cadence a burst of expiries can hold seats past their TTL during the exact minute they are wanted. Tighten the sweeper to 5 s on live on-sales, and expose expires_at so the client stops trusting the seat before the server does.
Best-available search is a scanGeneral admission at scale "Give me the best two seats together" reads a section's rows under lock. For reserved seating it is bounded; for a 100,000-seat festival tier it is a counter, and the design should say so and switch data models per event type rather than pretend one shape fits both.
One projector for every eventRoughly 5× events on sale at once A single consumer group painting every seat map is one slow partition away from lagging all of them. Give live on-sales their own consumer group and topic, and let the long tail share.
No multi-region write pathRegional failure Multi-AZ survives a datacentre; it does not survive a region. Active-active needs consensus on every seat, which is genuinely hard — so the honest answer is a warm standby with an RPO you state out loud and an on-sale you postpone rather than run degraded.
Cognito is a one-way doorAny migration Password hashes cannot be exported. Leaving means a forced reset or a lazy migration through a trigger. Priced and accepted, but it is the least reversible decision on the board.
If I had one more week: the waiting room, because it is the only item here that bites at the exact moment the business cares most — and every other weakness degrades gracefully while that one turns into a retry storm.

How you know it works

"How would you test this" is a question about the seat invariant, and the answer is not "unit tests"
  • Property tests on the invariant. Generate arbitrary interleavings of holds, expiries, payment successes and failures against a small venue; assert after every step that no seat has two live owners and that the seat_event log folds to the seat table. The bugs worth finding here are sequences nobody thought to write down.
  • A load test that is a fake on-sale. A million virtual fans, one event, one minute, against a staging inventory shard sized like production. The pass criterion is not latency — it is zero oversells and zero seats stuck HELD after the dust settles.
  • Testcontainers for the outbox path. Real Postgres, real Kafka, kill the relay mid-drain and assert the transition is published exactly once in effect. A mock cannot fail in the way that matters.
  • Replay a recorded on-sale. Take a day of production transitions, rebuild the seat map from scratch, and diff it against the live bitmap. This is the only test that proves the rebuild path actually works — and the rebuild path is your recovery plan.
  • Idempotency fuzzing. Fire every hold and every checkout twice, concurrently, with the same key. Assert one hold, one charge, one confirmation. Then do it with a network partition between the two.
  • Contract tests against the PSP. Recorded fixtures for succeeded, failed, disputed and — the one nobody records — the webhook that arrives twice and out of order.
The line to say: inventory has one invariant — a seat has at most one live owner and the log folds to the row — and everything else is a test of whether some path can violate it. That framing turns a vague question into a short answer.

How you ship it

Greenfield is the easy version. At an incumbent this runs alongside a ticketing core that already holds the seats
  1. Read-only shadow. Stand the new services up consuming the core's sales feed, building your own seat state and seat maps, serving nobody. Compare inventory nightly against the core's and fix the diffs until they are zero for a fortnight.
  2. Shadow the writes. Every hold goes to the core as it does today, and also to the new path. Neither the fan nor the seat is affected; you are only proving the two agree.
  3. Read cutover first. Point event pages and seat maps at your read model while the core is still authoritative. If it is wrong, a fan sees a stale colour rather than a lost seat — the cheapest possible way to be wrong.
  4. Write cutover by event. Small venues first, then a tier, then a tour. Never cut over an event mid-sale, and keep the reverse migration working the whole time, because a rollback you have never run is a hope.
  5. Strangle the core. Only once every hold flows through the new path does the old one get decommissioned — and the nightly inventory reconciliation against it stays running long after, because it is the cheapest correctness check you will ever have.
What makes this credible: at no point is there a flag day, and at every point the answer to “what if it is wrong” is a number you can already see.

Traps in this specific problem

Where candidates lose the room
  • Storing availability as a counter and decrementing it. Two holds race, the counter drifts, and you cannot say who owns seat 12A. For reserved seating the row is the seat.
  • Read-then-write in application code. SELECT status, check it is free, UPDATE. The gap between the check and the write is exactly where the second fan lives. The WHERE clause is the check.
  • Treating the PSP response as the payment. The create call returns in 300 ms with requires_action or processing, and the money has not moved. The seat is SOLD when the webhook says so — and the hold has to outlive that wait.
  • Forgetting hold expiry. Every abandoned cart is a seat nobody can buy. If the design has no clock-driven release, ask what the venue looks like ten minutes after the on-sale.
  • Letting a million clients poll the seat map. Snapshot once, then push deltas. Polling is the read tier you never needed to build.
  • Autoscaling as the on-sale plan. Capacity arrives after the spike is over. The on-sale time is on a calendar; pre-scale, cap retries at the edge, and name the waiting room as the lever after that.
  • Putting a card number anywhere near your services. The PSP's hosted field or SDK tokenizes in the browser. You store a payment-method token and stay out of PCI scope.
