Credit Card Portal

Cardholder-facing web and mobile portal: onboard, apply, view balances, pay the bill. Money moves, so the ledger is the product. Click through the phases the way you'd walk a whiteboard.

Spend the first five minutes cutting the problem down. A credit card portal touches issuing, authorization, fraud, collections, rewards, and disputes — you cannot design all of it, so say out loud what you are keeping and what you are deferring.

Functional requirements

The four flows to design end to end
  • Onboarding & auth. Register, verify identity, OIDC login, step-up MFA on money movement and profile changes, device binding to cut friction on repeat logins.
  • Card application. Submit an application, pull the credit bureau, run rules plus a model, decide in seconds: approve with a limit, decline with adverse-action reasons, or refer to manual review.
  • Account dashboard. Current balance, statement balance, credit limit, available credit, minimum due, due date, posted and pending transactions.
  • Payments. One-time or recurring payment from an external bank account over ACH, or an instant debit push. Schedule, cancel before cutoff, see it post.

The one-liner to open with

Say this before you draw a single box

"This is a read-heavy portal sitting on top of a write-critical ledger. Reads I'll scale with caching and a denormalized read model. Writes I'll keep small, strongly consistent, idempotent, and double-entry — I'd rather be slow than wrong on a cent. And I'll draw a hard boundary around cardholder data so most of my services stay out of PCI scope."

The in-scope capabilities as use cases, not features — who starts it, what the system promises when it finishes, and which outside party has to be involved. Say these out loud before you draw a single box: an interviewer who agrees with this list will agree with the architecture that follows.

Reading these diagrams

Standard UML, and worth being precise about — the two dashed relationships are the ones people get backwards
  • Stick figures are actors, and an actor is a role, not a person. The same human is an Applicant before approval and a Cardholder after.
  • Actors on the right are secondary — the system calls them, they don't call the system. Every one of them is a dependency you have to be able to fail without.
  • «include» points at behaviour that always happens. The arrow runs from the base use case to the included one. If it can be skipped, it isn't an include.
  • «extend» points the other way — from the optional behaviour back to the base, because the base doesn't know its extensions exist. Every extend has a condition attached.
  • The box is the system boundary, and drawing it is the point: everything outside is somebody else's uptime.
  • A time-triggered actor is still an actor. Settlement has no human initiator, which is exactly why candidates forget it exists.

Where the reads go

Peak read budget
  • Balance and available credit — cache hit, Redis, sub-5 ms
  • Recent 30 transactions — cache hit or read replica
  • Statement history — object storage, PDFs pre-rendered
  • Cache hit rate target: 90–95%, so only 5–10% of peak reads reach Postgres replicas

Where the writes go

Peak write budget
  • Authorizations from the network — highest volume, memo posts only
  • Settlements — nightly clearing files, batched
  • Cardholder payments — orders of magnitude smaller, roughly one per card per month
  • Fees, interest, refunds, chargebacks

The point of the estimate

Say this conclusion aloud

Portal payment writes are tiny. Authorization volume is what forces partitioning. So partition the ledger by account_id, keep every entry for one account on one shard, and you never need a distributed transaction for the operations that matter.

What needs partitioning, what needs sharding, and what needs neither

Two different problems that get the same word. Partitioning splits a table inside one database; sharding splits the data across databases. Reach for the second only when the first has run out.
TableGrowthStrategyWhy this key, and what it costs
ledger_entry
aurora-ledger
 Fastest in the system HASH (account_id) · 64 partitions Every money operation touches exactly one account, so hashing on account_id means no write ever spans a partition and the balance assertion stays inside one. The cost is that cross-account questions — "all fraud entries today" — fan out, which is fine because that question belongs to analytics, not to this database.
authorization · clearing

Both tables use the same key deliberately — a join across differently-partitioned tables is the thing that quietly ruins this design.

When partitioning is not enough

A partitioned table still lives in one writer. If that writer runs out of headroom, the next step is sharding by account across clusters — the same key, one level up. It works here for the same reason partitioning does: no transaction spans two accounts.

// routing is a pure function of the key, resolved before the connection is chosen
int shard = Math.floorMod(accountId.hashCode(), SHARD_COUNT);
DataSource ds = shards.get(shard);        // ledger-shard-03
  • Say what it costs before you are asked. Cross-shard queries stop existing, a shared unique constraint stops existing, and resharding means moving live accounts — so the shard count is chosen once and generously.
  • Use a virtual-bucket layer. Map accounts to 1,024 buckets and buckets to shards; growing then means moving buckets, not rehashing every account.
  • Do not shard the small tables. Accounts, terms and applications ride along with their service; only the ledger and transaction stores are candidates, because they are the only ones whose growth is unbounded.
The honest answer for this system: at 300M cardholders, partitioned Aurora with read replicas carries the write volume comfortably. Sharding is the plan you can describe, not the plan you start with — and knowing which of those you are proposing is most of the question.

Three schema decisions worth defending

  • Money is bigint minor units, never a float and never a decimal that someone will read into a double on the way out.
  • Direction plus a positive amount, never a signed column. A sign bug silently doubles a balance and no constraint catches it.
  • Balance is derived. The snapshot is a cache with a version, rebuildable by folding the entries, and a nightly job proves it still matches.
  • Every outbox lives beside the data it describes, so the event and the row commit together or neither exists.
  • Cross-service references are plain uuids, not foreign keys. The row lives in another service's database, and pretending otherwise is how a boundary quietly dissolves.
  • Partial indexes on the hot set, so a table that accumulates history keeps a working set the size of the live work.

Command Query Responsibility Segregation is one sentence: the model you write with and the model you read from are different models, kept in step by an event rather than by a shared table. Everything else about it is consequence.

The split, drawn out

Two models, one event between them, and no call in either direction

How it is used here

Not as an abstraction — as three concrete pieces you can point at
SideComponentsWhat it optimises for
Command Payment · Ledger · Transactions · Account, each with its own Aurora cluster Correctness at commit. Normalized tables, row locks, unique constraints, and an append-only entry log. Roughly 600 writes a second, and every one of them has to be right.
The seam Outbox table → relay → MSK One event per committed change, published from the same transaction that wrote the row. Neither side calls the other, and neither imports the other's model.
Query Dashboard BFF · ElastiCache · read replicas · Reporting Service · DynamoDB Answering the exact question the screen asks. Denormalized, pre-joined, pre-aggregated. Roughly 30,000 reads a second, none of which touch the write path.
Read the ratio again: 30,000 reads against 600 writes. Serving both from one model means sizing the correctness-critical path for the volume of the trivial one. The split is not elegance — it is what makes the expensive guarantees affordable.

What it buys

  • Independent scaling. Add cache nodes and replicas without touching the primary. The write side scales on transaction volume, the read side on eyeballs, and those two numbers move for completely different reasons.
  • Different consistency per side. Strong where money is decided, eventual where a number is displayed — with the staleness stated rather than hidden.
  • Different storage shapes. The write model is normalized because invariants live in relationships. The read model is denormalized because a dashboard is one shape. Neither compromise is forced on the other.
  • Failure isolation. Losing the read model degrades latency; losing the write model stops writes. One of those is an incident and the other is a page — and the split is what keeps them separate.
  • The read model is disposable. Wrong aggregate, new field, changed shape — reset the consumer group and replay. Rebuildable state is the underrated half of this pattern.
  • Query shapes can change freely. Adding a spend-by-category view meant a new consumer and a new store, and zero changes to the ledger.

What it costs, said before you are asked

  • Eventual consistency becomes a product decision. Someone has to answer "how stale is acceptable" with a number, and the UI has to show an asOf timestamp rather than implying the figure is live.
  • Read-your-own-writes needs an explicit exception. After a payment the customer must see it, so the Payment Service invalidates the summary key synchronously before returning 201. One deliberate crack, for exactly one case.
  • More moving parts. An outbox, a relay, a topic, a consumer, and a projection per read model — all of which need lag monitoring and a DLQ.
  • Two schemas to evolve. A field added to the write model is not automatically in the read model, and the rebuild has to be part of the deploy rather than an afterthought.
When not to use it: if reads and writes have the same shape and similar volume, CQRS is ceremony. It earns its keep at a ratio like this one, or when the read shape is genuinely different from the write shape. Saying that unprompted is worth more than the pattern name.

In code, the split is two packages that share nothing

// command side — invariants, one aggregate, one transaction
@Transactional
public TransactionId handle(PostEntries cmd) {
    BalanceSnapshot snap = repo.lockForUpdate(cmd.accountId());   // strong, serialised
    assertBalanced(cmd.legs());                                   // sums to zero or refuse
    entries.appendAll(cmd.legs());
    snap.apply(cmd.legs());
    outbox.put(new EntryPosted(cmd));                          // same tx, no dual write
    return cmd.transactionId();
}

// query side — no domain objects, no invariants, just the shape the screen wants
@Cacheable(value = "summary", key = "#accountId")
public AccountSummaryView summary(UUID accountId) {
    return jdbc.queryForObject(SUMMARY_SQL, VIEW_MAPPER, accountId);
}

// the seam — the only thing that connects them
@KafkaListener(topics = "ledger.entry-posted.v1")
public void on(EntryPosted e) {
    cache.evict("summary", e.accountId());       // idempotent: evicting twice is free
}

The tell that it is real: the query side has no @Transactional, no aggregate, and no way to change anything. If your read path can write, you have two models and one of them is lying.

Reading the diagram out loud

What each convention encodes — shape first, then colour, then line style
  • Solid arrows are synchronous calls — the caller blocks and owns the timeout.
  • Dashed arrows are responses or asynchronous returns that arrive later, sometimes days later.
  • Arrows point the way the call goes, not the way the data goes. The outbox relay points at the relational databases because it polls them. The one exception is DynamoDB Streams, which genuinely pushes — and is drawn pointing outward for exactly that reason.
  • Nothing writes to the ledger database directly except the Ledger Service. That is the single most important line on the board.
  • No service dual-writes to a database and Kafka. Everything goes database → outbox → relay → Kafka.
  • The two dashed bands at the bottom are not services. Observability and disaster recovery apply to every box above them, so they are drawn as bands rather than nodes — a node with no edges would be a lie about how it is wired.
  • The outbox is drawn inside the database, because that is where it lives — a table committed in the same transaction as the row it describes. The relay reads it; it is not a queue sitting between the two.
  • Every store sits directly opposite the one service that owns it. If you can draw a horizontal line between a service and a database, that service is the only thing connecting to it — no shared clusters, no second writer.
  • Green boxes marked AWS are managed services — someone else runs the failover. Everything else is code you deploy and page for.
  • The red box is the whole PCI scope. Everything else handles tokens, so it stays out of the audit.
  • Select a flow and an amber dashed box appears around the components only that flow uses. Everything left outside it is shared — which is exactly the set whose blast radius crosses flows, and the set you cannot change alone.
  • Kafka is partitioned by account_id, which is why per-account ordering holds without a global lock.

The dead letter path

What happens to a message that will not process — and the harder question of what happens to the ones behind it

Transient — goes to the retry ladder

  • Timeouts, connection resets, 5xx from a downstream, broker unavailability, a lock contention rollback.
  • Non-blocking retry: the message moves to a separate retry topic with its own consumer and a backoff. It never sleeps in the handler, because a sleeping consumer holds a partition hostage and everything behind it stops.
  • Backoff is exponential with jitter. A synchronized retry storm is how a struggling downstream becomes a dead one.

Permanent — straight to the DLT

  • Deserialization failures, schema incompatibility, validation errors, a referenced aggregate that does not exist.
  • No retries at all. Burning six attempts on a message that can never parse is theatre — it delays the alert and fills the ladder with garbage.
  • The classifier is an explicit exception-to-decision map, not a catch-all. Anything unclassified is treated as permanent, because a mystery failure retried forever is worse than one that surfaces.

Operating it

  • One DLT per consumer group, never a shared bin. A shared queue means you cannot tell whose problem it is or replay one team's messages without replaying everyone's.
  • Alarm on depth and age. Depth catches a burst. Age catches the single message from four days ago that nobody noticed — the more dangerous of the two.
  • Every dead-lettered message carries its own forensics in headers: original topic, partition, offset, exception class and message, attempt count, and the correlation ID that ties it back to the originating trace.
  • Replay is re-publishing to the source topic, not hand-patching state. It is only safe because consumers are idempotent by event ID — which is the reason that property is non-negotiable upstream.
  • The ordering trade-off is the real answer. Dead-lettering event 3 and processing event 4 breaks per-account order. For notifications that is fine — a receipt arriving before a return notice is survivable.
  • For the ledger it is not. A ledger consumer that cannot process an entry halts its partition and pages rather than skipping ahead. Availability of the read path is worth less than the correctness of a balance.

Observability — the eight-second payment

One incident, walked end to end. The tooling, coverage and alerting policy are on the Observability tab.

One trace, pulled by correlation ID from the payment the customer complained about. Every span is real instrumentation you have to have decided on in advance — after the incident is too late.

Metrics — what you alert on

  • RED per endpoint — rate, errors, duration as histograms, never averages. A p99 is a number; an average is a rumour.
  • Prometheus scrapes, Grafana asks. Micrometer exposes the registry, Prometheus stores it, Grafana holds the dashboards and the alert rules. CloudWatch keeps the AWS-side metrics — RDS connections, MSK consumer lag, Aurora failover events — that no application can report about itself.
  • Four that are specific to this system: outbox lag in rows and seconds, consumer group lag per topic, DLQ depth and age, and the reconciliation break count with its dollar total.
  • Saturation, not just latency — connection pool wait time is the metric that would have caught the trace above before a customer did.

Logs — structured, and safe

  • JSON to stdout, one event per line, with traceId, spanId, accountId, and idempotencyKey on every record.
  • Never a PAN, an SSN, a token that maps to either, or a full request body from the payments path. Enforce it with a serializer that redacts by field name, not by developer discipline.
  • Retention split: 30 days hot for debugging, seven years in WORM for anything the ledger or a decision touched.

Traces — across the async gap

  • OpenTelemetry is the instrumentation layer — auto-instrumented for HTTP and JDBC, manual spans around the vault, the rail, and the scoring call. The collector then fans out: traces to AWS X-Ray, metrics to Prometheus, logs to CloudWatch. Instrument once against a standard rather than three times against three vendors.
  • Context propagates through Kafka headers, so the consumer's span links back to the request that produced the event. Without this, every trace stops at the outbox and async debugging becomes guesswork.
  • Tail-based sampling: keep 100% of errors and anything over the SLO, 1% of the rest. Head sampling throws away exactly the traces you need.

What pages, and what doesn't

  • Page on symptoms. SLO burn rate on the payment write path, error-budget exhaustion, a missed ACH cutoff, and any reconciliation break over a threshold. All four mean a customer is affected or money is unaccounted for.
  • Ticket on causes. A pod restart, a rising cache miss rate, a slow query. Paging on causes is how you train an on-call rotation to ignore alerts.
  • Every alert names an owner and links a runbook. An alert nobody owns is a dashboard with a siren attached.
  • The reconciliation break is the highest-signal alert in the system — it is the only one that can tell you your ledger and the network disagree, which no latency metric ever will.

The four that carry the design

If you only defend a few choices, defend these — the rest are conventional
  • Aurora, for the ledger. The invariant is two legs summing to zero, asserted before commit, and a balance read under a row lock. That is a relational engine's job description. Every "why not DynamoDB for everything" question ends here.
  • MSK, for the seam. Retention is what makes the read model disposable. With a queue, a projection bug means data you cannot recover; with a log, it means a consumer group you reset.
  • RDS Proxy, quietly. Nobody asks about it and it is the difference between scaling pods and running out of connections. Naming it unprompted is a strong signal you have operated Postgres under load.
  • Cognito, with the caveat attached. Buying identity is right for greenfield — and you should say in the same breath that password hashes cannot be exported, so it is the least reversible decision on the board.

Portability, since someone always asks

Roughly 80% of this design ports. Postgres, Kafka, Redis and S3-compatible storage exist everywhere; the services behind them are ordinary Spring Boot. The lock-in is concentrated in four places, and it is worth knowing which:

  • Cognito — the real one. Hashes cannot be exported, so leaving means a forced reset or a lazy migration.
  • DynamoDB — the item model and conditional expressions would need rewriting, though the data itself is derived and rebuildable.
  • IAM and Lattice — the authorization model, not the code, is what would have to be rebuilt.
  • Aurora's storage layer — plain Postgres runs the same schema, but you take back failover, backups and replica lag as your problem.

Five tools, and the answer to the only question that matters when something is slow: which one do you open first? Instrument once with OpenTelemetry, then let each backend do the thing it is actually good at.

Tracing across the asynchronous gap

The part most designs get wrong, and the reason the traces are worth having at all
// producing: the trace context rides in the Kafka record headers
ProducerRecord<String, byte[]> rec = new ProducerRecord<>(topic, accountId, payload);
propagator.inject(Context.current(), rec.headers(),
    (h, k, v) -> h.add(k, v.getBytes(UTF_8)));      // traceparent, tracestate

// consuming: link the consumer span back to the request that caused it
Context parent = propagator.extract(Context.root(), record.headers(), GETTER);
Span span = tracer.spanBuilder("ledger.entry-posted")
                  .setParent(parent)
                  .setSpanKind(CONSUMER)
                  .startSpan();

Without this, every trace stops at the outbox. You would see a 201 returned in 40 ms and have no way to connect it to the notification that never arrived twenty minutes later. With it, one correlation ID spans the write, the relay, the topic, the consumer and the side effect.

What pages, and what does not

  • Page on symptoms. SLO burn on the payment write path, error-budget exhaustion, a missed ACH cutoff, and any reconciliation break over a threshold. Each one means a customer is affected or money is unaccounted for.
  • The reconciliation break is the highest-signal alert in the system — the only one that can tell you your ledger and the network disagree, which no latency metric will ever say.
  • Ticket on causes. A pod restart, a rising cache miss rate, a slow query. Paging on causes is how you train an on-call rotation to ignore alerts.
  • Every alert names an owner and links a runbook. An alert nobody owns is a dashboard with a siren attached.
Not on the list: CPU, memory, pod count. Those are causes, and causes belong in a dashboard you open after a symptom has already paged you.

Smaller patterns worth naming in passing

Not worth a deep dive, but dropping the name shows you've built this before
  • Snapshotting. A memoized fold over the entry log. It's what makes an event-sourced ledger queryable in single-digit milliseconds instead of replaying seven years of history.
  • Compensating Transaction. A returned ACH payment is corrected with new reversing entries, never an UPDATE or a DELETE. In finance you don't undo — you post the opposite.
  • Bulkhead. Sits beside every circuit breaker. A slow bureau consumes a bounded pool instead of starving balance reads.
  • Dead Letter Queue. With a retry topic and backoff in front of it. A DLQ nobody monitors is just a slower way to lose messages.
  • Shadow / Dark Launch. New fraud models score alongside production for weeks before they earn the authority to challenge anyone.
  • Rate Limiting. Sliding window at the gateway, per user and per IP, with write budgets far tighter than read budgets.

Everything an interviewer asks after the design holds up: what is wrong with it, how do you know it works, how do you ship it, and what does it cost. Volunteering these reads as confidence; being asked for them and having nothing reads as the opposite.

What I would change, and what breaks at 10×

Said before anyone asks. Each one is a real weakness in the design as drawn, not a humble-brag
The weaknessBites atWhat I would do about it
Stand-in approves blindAny ledger outage If the ledger is unreachable during an authorization we approve under a floor limit, which is unsecured exposure we never underwrote. Bound it: a per-account floor cached ahead of time, a global kill switch, and a reconciliation job that finds every stand-in approval and posts it the moment the ledger returns.
Reporting has no backfill pathFirst schema change Aggregates rebuild by replaying the topic, but the topic only retains seven days. Adding a new grain or category means the history is simply missing. Fix by keeping a compacted snapshot of entries in S3 and rebuilding from there rather than from Kafka.
One relay for every outboxRoughly 5× volume A single relay polling five databases is one process away from being the bottleneck and the single point of failure for every projection. Split it per source database, or move to Debezium per cluster — same pattern, more instances.
Balance snapshot is a hot row10× authorizations Every authorization for an account serialises on one row. Fine per account, until a commercial account with thousands of cards arrives. Then you need per-card sub-accounts that roll up, and the invariant moves with them.
No multi-region write pathRegional failure Multi-AZ survives a datacentre; it does not survive a region. Active-active needs conflict resolution on a balance, which is genuinely hard — so the honest answer is warm standby with an RPO you state out loud, not a claim of active-active.
Cognito is a one-way doorAny migration Password hashes cannot be exported. Leaving means a forced reset or a lazy migration through a trigger. Priced and accepted, but it is the least reversible decision on the board.
If I had one more week: the backfill path, because it is the only item here that gets harder the longer it is left — every day without it is another day of history you cannot reconstruct.

How you know it works

"How would you test this" is a question about the ledger, and the answer is not "unit tests"
  • Property tests on the invariant. Generate arbitrary sequences of authorizations, clearings, payments and reversals; assert after every one that entries sum to the snapshot and that no balance was reached by an UPDATE. The bugs worth finding here are sequences nobody thought to write down.
  • Testcontainers for the outbox path. Real Postgres, real Kafka, kill the relay mid-drain and assert the event is published exactly once in effect. A mock cannot fail in the way that matters.
  • Contract tests against every adapter. Stripe and the card network are recorded fixtures — including the failure shapes, which are the ones nobody records. Pact or golden files, run in CI, so a vendor change breaks the build rather than production.
  • Replay a recorded day. Take a day of production events, rebuild a read model from scratch, and diff it against the live one. This is the only test that proves the rebuild path actually works — and the rebuild path is your recovery plan.
  • Idempotency fuzzing. Fire every write twice, concurrently, with the same key. Assert one row, one event, one charge. Then do it with a network partition in between.
  • Reconciliation as a test in CI. The same three-way comparison that runs nightly, run against a seeded dataset with deliberate breaks. If the control cannot catch a planted error, it will not catch a real one.
The line to say: the ledger has one invariant — entries sum to zero and to the snapshot — and everything else is a test of whether some path can violate it. That framing turns a vague question into a short answer.

How you ship it

Greenfield is the easy version. At an incumbent this runs alongside a core that already holds the money
  1. Read-only shadow. Stand the new services up consuming the core's events, building your own ledger, serving nobody. Compare balances nightly against the core's and fix the diffs until they are zero for a fortnight.
  2. Shadow the writes. Every payment goes to the core as it does today, and also to the new path. Neither the customer nor the money is affected; you are only proving the two agree.
  3. Read cutover first. Point the dashboard at your read model while the core is still authoritative. If it is wrong, the customer sees a stale number rather than a lost payment — the cheapest possible way to be wrong.
  4. Write cutover by cohort. New accounts first, then a percentage, then by product. Keep the reverse migration working the whole time, because a rollback you have never run is a hope.
  5. Strangle the core. Only once every write flows through the new path does the old one get decommissioned — and the reconciliation against it stays running long after, because it is the cheapest correctness check you will ever have.
What makes this credible: at no point is there a flag day, and at every point the answer to “what if it is wrong” is a number you can already see.

Traps in this specific problem

Where candidates lose the room
  • Storing balance as a mutable column and updating it. Concurrent auths race, the number drifts, and you can't explain a statement. Derive it from entries plus a snapshot.
  • Using floats for money. Long cents or BigDecimal, always. Say it before you're asked.
  • Treating a payment as instant. The Stripe call returns in milliseconds with status processing and the money has not moved. A payment is PENDING, then POSTED, and may fail days later — that reversal is a new entry, never a delete.
  • Putting the PAN anywhere outside the vault. Logs, Kafka payloads, cache keys, analytics. Tokenize at the edge.
  • Ignoring the business date. A payment at 11:58pm on the 30th posts to the 30th even if it processes at 12:03am. Due-date credit depends on it.
  • Forgetting reconciliation. Every external party needs a daily job that compares your ledger against theirs — Stripe balance transactions, network clearing files — and raises a break with a dollar amount and an owner.
  • Letting an approval create the account. The customer created it; approval attaches a credit line. Getting that backwards forces Decisioning to call Account Service synchronously, and then an Account outage fails applications.
  • Confusing a hold with a transaction. An authorization moves pending and available credit. Only the clearing moves the current balance, and it routinely arrives for a different amount.
